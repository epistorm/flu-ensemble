Overview

The Epistorm Ensemble produces weekly probabilistic forecasts of influenza hospital admissions for all 50 US states, Washington DC, and the US nationally. Forecasts are generated each week for horizons of 1 to 4 weeks ahead and submitted to the CDC's FluSight Forecast Hub. This dashboard provides an interactive view of those forecasts along with evaluation metrics that compare performance against a baseline model and other contributing forecasters.

The forecasts are built from individual model submissions to the FluSight Forecast Hub, and the observed admissions shown alongside them come from the FluSight target data (NHSN). Everything is refreshed automatically each Thursday: the pipeline fetches the latest model submissions, builds the ensemble forecasts, computes evaluation scores, and redeploys this dashboard.

Ensemble Methods

Two ensemble methods are available on the dashboard:

Median Ensemble

For each quantile level, the ensemble value is the median across all contributing models' predictions at that quantile. This is a modified form of the Vincent averaging technique (Vincent, 1912; Howerton et al., 2023). Using the median rather than the mean makes it robust to outliers and produces well-calibrated intervals.

Linear Opinion Pool (LOP) Ensemble

The LOP method (Stone, 1961; Howerton et al., 2023) combines the full cumulative distribution functions (CDFs) of each model rather than individual quantiles. For a given value, each model's CDF is evaluated and the results are averaged, producing a blended probability distribution. This approach better captures multi-modal uncertainty and model disagreement.

Both methods produce forecasts at 23 quantile levels (0.01 to 0.99), which are used to derive prediction intervals, trend categories, and activity levels. Forecast Outputs

Quantile Forecasts

The primary output is a set of quantile predictions for weekly influenza hospital admissions. These are visualized as fan charts on the dashboard, showing the median forecast along with 50% and 95% prediction intervals.

Trend Categories

Trend forecasts classify the expected change in hospitalizations into five categories: large decrease, decrease, stable, increase, and large increase. These are derived from the quantile forecasts by computing the probability that the change relative to the most recent observation falls within horizon-specific thresholds based on per-capita rates. This categorical-trend framework and its horizon-specific thresholds follow the approach of Mathis et al., 2025, and a multi-season evaluation of these trend forecasts is presented in Davis et al., 2026.

How trend probabilities are read from the forecast

The forecast fan (median with 50% and 90% prediction intervals) is compared with the last reported week. Bands measured from that week — stable, increase/decrease, and large — mark how big the change is; their boundaries come from the per-100,000 rate change and widen further ahead (a floor keeps changes under ~10 admissions Stable). For the selected week, the share of the forecast in each band is the probability of that trend; exact bounds for a state appear on the dashboard hover. Drag the forecast, spread, or week to explore.

Activity Levels

Activity level forecasts classify the expected hospitalization level into four categories: Low, Moderate, High, and Very High. Thresholds are defined for each location using the moving epidemic method (Vega et al., 2013) applied to historical influenza hospitalization data. Activity level probabilities are computed from the forecast CDF evaluated at each threshold.

How activity-level probabilities are read from the forecast

The forecast is shown as a fan — the median with its 50% and 90% prediction intervals, widening further ahead. A state's moving-epidemic-method thresholds (the real United States national values) split the admissions axis into the four activity bands. For the selected week, the share of the forecast that falls in each band is the probability of that level, and the gauge points to the most likely one. Drag the forecast, spread, or week to see the intervals and chances shift.

Evaluation Methods

Weighted Interval Score (WIS)

The Weighted Interval Score (Bracher et al., 2021) is a proper scoring rule for quantile forecasts that measures both sharpness (how narrow the prediction intervals are) and calibration (whether observations fall within the predicted intervals at the expected rate). Lower WIS indicates better forecast accuracy.

For a single $(1-\alpha)$ prediction interval with lower bound $l$ and upper bound $u$, the interval score given an observation $y$ is:

$\text{IS}_\alpha(l, u, y) = (u - l) + \frac{2}{\alpha}(l - y)\,\mathbf{1}\{y < l\} + \frac{2}{\alpha}(y - u)\,\mathbf{1}\{y > u\}$

The first term rewards sharpness (narrower intervals), while the penalty terms penalize observations that fall outside the interval. The WIS aggregates this across $K = 11$ symmetric prediction intervals and the median forecast $m$:

$\text{WIS} = \frac{1}{K + \frac{1}{2}} \left[ \sum_{k=1}^{K} \frac{\alpha_k}{2} \cdot \text{IS}_{\alpha_k} + \frac{1}{2} \left| m - y \right| \right]$

Scoring a forecast with WIS

The WIS scores a probabilistic forecast against what actually happened, rewarding forecasts that are both sharp (narrow) and well-calibrated (the outcome stays inside the intervals). Lower is better. Drag the outcome, or change the sharpness or week.

The forecast is a median wrapped in nested prediction intervals (50–95%). The outcome is what actually happened that week.

Each interval adds its width plus a penalty if the outcome lands outside it. Inner intervals carry the largest weight, so they contribute most.

WIS is the weighted average of those interval scores (plus the median error), regrouped as dispersion + over‑ + under‑prediction.

WIS is an absolute score: its width and penalty terms are measured in the same units as the forecast, so its magnitude scales with the size of the values being predicted. Every model scores a far larger WIS near the winter peak than in the quiet shoulders of the season, which makes raw scores hard to compare across weeks or locations. To put them on a common footing we use relative WIS — each model's mean score divided by the FluSight-baseline's.

Why we use relative WIS

WIS is measured in admissions, so its size depends on how big the numbers are, not just how good the forecast is. Dividing a model's WIS by the baseline's strips that out, giving a score you can compare across weeks and locations.

$\text{relative WIS} = \textcolor{#1f7a8c}{\text{WIS}_{\text{model}}}\, / \,\textcolor{#bf8b30}{\text{WIS}_{\text{baseline}}}$

Across a season the model and the flat baseline each issue a 4‑week forecast. The baseline holds the last value flat; the model follows the recent trend.

Each forecast's actual WIS (bars) runs largest where observed admissions (black line) are biggest — the score tracks the magnitude of the data, so raw WIS can't be compared across weeks.

Relative WIS divides the model's WIS by the baseline's, removing the magnitude. Below 1 the model wins; above 1 the baseline wins — as at the peak, where the model overshoots the turn.

Prediction Interval Coverage

Coverage measures calibration — whether prediction intervals contain the observed value at the expected rate (Cramer et al., 2022). For a given interval level $(1-\alpha)$, coverage is simply the fraction of forecasts where the observation falls within the interval:

$\text{Coverage}_{1-\alpha} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbf{1}\left\{ l_i^{(\alpha)} \leq y_i \leq u_i^{(\alpha)} \right\}$

Is the model well calibrated?

Coverage asks whether the model's uncertainty is honest: does its X% prediction interval really contain the outcome about X% of the time? Play a season to see the coverage averaged across every week's forecasts, or switch the model's confidence and highlight a different interval.

Evaluation Controls

The evaluations page allows filtering by:

  • Aggregation period: Full season, last 2 weeks, or last 4 weeks
  • Forecast horizon: All horizons or individual weeks (1–4)
  • Location: Hover or click on states to see location-specific metrics
